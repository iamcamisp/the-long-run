Issue #1 Week of May 28, 2026

Energy shocks, embedded rates, and the great tech bifurcation

This week, three stories collided. Brazil's central bank admitted its inflation anchors are slipping even as it cuts rates, the US and China effectively split the semiconductor supply chain into two incompatible systems, and the AI industry moved decisively from models to enterprise deployment. All three expose the same problem: when you're dealing with structural shocks and geopolitical divides, monetary policy and trade law act like antibiotics on a resistant infection. Let us explain why.

🇧🇷 Brazil

Brazil's inflation trap: cutting rates while expectations spiral

The Copom is easing into a fire it cannot control. The Central Bank cut the Selic again last week, but the market's inflation expectations have now risen for eleven consecutive weeks, breaking through the official ceiling and signaling that monetary policy has lost its grip on price setting behavior.

The story is deceptively simple. The Brazilian Central Bank delivered a Copom statement hawkish in tone on Tuesday, May 5, 2026, warning that the duration of the Iran-United States conflict could force the Comitê de Política Monetária to slow or pause the easing cycle that lowered the Selic policy rate by 25 basis points to 14.50 percent on April 29. Yet even as the committee spoke, the market was repricing upward. The weekly Focus survey of economists lifted the median 2026 consumer price estimate to 5.04 percent from 4.92 percent, the eleventh consecutive rise.

That last number is the real news. That keeps the projection above the 4.5 percent upper bound of the target, whose center is 3 percent with a 1.5-point tolerance, signaling expectations remain unanchored despite firmer central bank messaging. When a central bank cuts rates but inflation expectations simultaneously rise for eleven straight weeks, something has broken in the transmission mechanism. It is not a lack of toughness; it is a loss of credibility.

The mechanism is oil. Brent crude traded between 110 and 114 dollars per barrel during the meeting window, well above the institution's prior 80 dollar baseline. The shock feeds into Brazilian inflation through gasoline, diesel, freight and aviation fuel, which Petrobras already raised by 18 percent on May 1. But the real damage is deeper. Services inflation is the single component the Copom watches most closely because it captures wage pass-through and domestic demand pressure that monetary policy can actually move. And there, the committee is losing ground. Itaú's three-month annualized seasonally adjusted core services measure accelerated from 5.4% to 6.0%, the data point that effectively closes the door on faster Copom cuts.

Here is the trap. The Central Bank is right to cut rates. The Bank assessed a cut was still called for, as the cooling of GDP growth indicated the transmission of prolonged elevated rates to the real economy. But cutting rates into a supply shock (oil) and a wage setting shock (services) sends a contradictory signal to the market: the bank is either panicked about growth, or it cannot control inflation, or both. None of those stories end well for inflation expectations.

The market has taken its answer. Focus survey participants now expect a year-end Selic of 13 percent, up from 12.50 percent a month earlier. That is a subtle but crucial shift. The Copom is cutting; the market is pricing a shorter cycle and a higher terminal rate. The real interest rate, which had been extraordinarily high at around 8 to 9 percent in April, is now expected to stay stubbornly elevated. This is not a victory for rate cuts. It is a failure of forward guidance.

What happens next depends on oil. The committee also flagged second-round effects on global supply chains, which the minutes describe as a more lasting source of inflation pressure into 2028. If the Strait of Hormuz reopens and Brent falls back toward 80 dollars, the market will breathe and expectations may reanchor. If not, the Copom faces a choice between cutting into an inflation spiral or holding firm and risking a sharper economic slowdown. Both roads lead somewhere bad. That is why today's decision matters so much and says so little.

Why it matters

Brazil's real interest rate is set to stay punishingly high through the year, not because the Copom is stubborn, but because oil and wage inflation have decoupled monetary policy from inflation expectations. With an election year ahead and fiscal pressures mounting, the central bank has lost room to maneuver. Watch the real currency rate and capital flows.

The theory underneath

The expectations channel and the limits of monetary credibility in open economies

The mainstream view

The central bank can anchor inflation expectations through a credible commitment to a nominal anchor and a demonstrated track record. As long as inflation targeting regimes maintain independence, market participants will believe in future price stability and set wages and prices accordingly, making inflation a choice, not a shock response.

The heterodox view

In an open economy facing commodity price shocks and labor market tightness, expectations are shaped by visible cost pressures and wage setting power, not by central bank announcements. When workers and firms see oil and services inflation rising, they will set wages and prices higher regardless of the nominal rate path. Monetary policy becomes rearguard action rather than control.

Where we land: Brazil's Copom is caught between a shortage of real resources (oil at $110+) and a breakdown in wage restraint (services at 6%). The nominal rate matters far less than the oil price and the labor market. Until one of those constraints eases, rate cuts will be absorbed into higher inflation expectations rather than lower borrowing costs for growth.

💻 Technology🌍 International

The chip war has already bifurcated the semiconductor market, and export controls didn't stop it

The US has effectively lost the ability to slow China's AI capability through chip exports. Rather than slowing China, restrictions have accelerated a structural split in which both countries are building incompatible semiconductor ecosystems. The competition has moved from access to independence.

The conventional reading of US semiconductor export controls is that they work: deny China the best chips, and you slow Chinese AI. That was the theory behind restrictions on NVIDIA and advanced node manufacturing. A US export licence to ship the more capable H200 chip to China was granted in February 2026, but Nvidia has generated no revenue from it. Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick confirmed the chips have not been delivered. The policy is a shield. Except it isn't working.

What is happening instead is a full bifurcation of the semiconductor supply chain. On the US side, there is NVIDIA, TSMC, Samsung, and advanced node dominance. On the China side, there is Huawei developing its Ascend AI chips, SMIC building foundry capacity at mature nodes, and a growing ecosystem of domestic equipment and materials suppliers. The two are no longer competing in the same market. They are building incompatible systems.

The numbers tell the story. Huawei's AI chip revenue is projected to surge 60% year on year to US$12 billion in 2026, and Cambricon posted record profits of 1.04 billion yuan in the first half of 2025 on revenue that surged over 4,000% year on year. Goldman Sachs expects Cambricon's AI chip shipments to grow from 143,000 units in 2025 to 2.1 million by 2030. These are not workaround numbers. These are companies building production at scale for a closed market.

The wafer supply chain shows the pattern most clearly. The company leading this expansion is Xi'an Eswin Material Technology, the wafer-making arm of Beijing ESWIN Technology Group. According to TechNews, Eswin is simultaneously building out facilities in Xi'an and Wuhan, targeting 1.2 million 12-inch wafer starts per month in combined capacity this year alone, enough to serve roughly 40% of China's domestic 12-inch wafer demand and push Eswin's own global market share above 10%. That is not incremental progress. That is a structural ramp.

The deeper point is this: US policy has not made China poorer or slower. It has made China more self-sufficient. What started as a targeted export control regime has evolved into something far more complex, a bifurcated global chip ecosystem where both sides are building independent supply chains, independent chip architectures, and increasingly, incompatible AI infrastructure. The cost is real, Chinese fabs use older process nodes and burn more power, but they are scaled and building. The semiconductor market of 2026 is not one with a gatekeeper. It is two incompatible ecosystems competing at different nodes for different customers.

What does this mean for the rest of the world? It means Europe and smaller Asian economies face a choice: buy American or buy Chinese, but not both. As tech and chip majors continue to pursue this new form of vertical integration (referred to as circular financing by some industry analysts), the semiconductor industry's capital allocation strategies may need to shift from capacity- to capability-driven models, with an emphasis on achieving AI system-level differentiation. In the midst of these developments, Europe appears caught between US export controls (restricting advanced chip sales to China) and China's countermeasures. The next phase will not be about export controls. It will be about ecosystem lock in and which allied nations accept which supply chain.

NVIDIA's CEO Jensen Huang said it plainly: the US policy had backfired. The question is not whether China can build chips. It is whether the West has the time and the market power to make that choice matter before two incompatible systems are locked in place. On current trajectories, that window is closing.

Why it matters

The semiconductor market is structurally realigning into two incompatible systems. This is not a temporary phase caused by export controls; it is a permanent feature of great power competition in chips. Companies and nations must now choose ecosystem affiliation. The winners will be those that move fastest to lock in allied customers and suppliers, not those that hope for control over a unified global market.

The theory underneath

Industrial policy and the fragmentation of global supply chains

The mainstream view

Trade restrictions and export controls slow the diffusion of technology and maintain comparative advantage in high-value sectors. By restricting advanced chip sales to China, the US maintains leadership in frontier AI capability and forces competitors to accept lower performance or pay a cost premium, preserving US dominance in AI infrastructure.

The heterodox view

Once restrictions are applied, the targeted economy has strong incentives to build alternative supply chains using domestic resources, lower-cost labor, and state support. Export controls accelerate the shift from a single global market to segmented, parallel systems, each optimizing for cost and political control rather than performance. Both sides lose the efficiency gains of an open market, but both achieve self-sufficiency at a cost. The net effect is not slowed Chinese AI but bifurcated global infrastructure.

Where we land: US export controls have not slowed China's AI advancement; they have accelerated a split in the semiconductor supply chain into two incompatible systems. The policy is internally consistent (deny China the best chips) but strategically incomplete (it does not prevent China from building a second-best system at scale). The real competition is now over ecosystem lock in, not technology access.

💻 Technology
The idea: Platform power and vertical integration in AI infrastructure

Enterprise AI is now the competitive battleground, not models, but deployment

The two biggest stories from May that missed most daily coverage hit us at once: KPMG deployed Claude to 276,000 employees across 138 countries, and OpenAI launched a $4 billion consulting subsidiary called DeployCo. Between them, these announcements signal that the AI industry's most important structural shift is not happening in benchmarks or model releases -- it is happening in the battle for who controls enterprise AI deployment.

The competition is no longer about which model is fastest or most capable in isolation. KPMG Global Chairman and CEO Bill Thomas framed the alliance around "security, trust, and governance rather than speed alone" -- which is the right enterprise pitch in 2026. When your AI vendor is also Anthropic (the company that litigated against the DoD over autonomous weapons and published the first public AI safety framework), the governance framing is credible. The winners will be the firms that can embed AI into enterprise workflows at scale while offering the governance and control that large organizations demand. That is a different game than model leaderboards.

Why it matters

The AI industry's value migration is complete: from model development to enterprise integration. Companies that can deploy AI at scale into existing workflows while managing risk and governance will capture more value than those that only build models. This shifts the competitive advantage toward consulting firms, systems integrators, and AI companies with deep enterprise sales.

🇺🇸 USA
The idea: External constraints on monetary policy

The Fed is on pause. Oil shocks and geopolitical risk are all that matter now.

Inflation remains somewhat elevated. The Committee seeks to achieve maximum employment and inflation at the rate of 2 percent over the longer run. The Fed's median projection still points to one quarter-point cut in 2026, even after officials raised their inflation forecasts. In plain terms, the Fed will hold rates steady through most of 2026.

Aggressive rate increases from early 2022 to mid-2023 helped lower core PCE inflation from a peak above 5.5% year-over-year in 2022 to 3.0% in February 2026. The Fed later shifted course as inflation slowed, cutting its target interest rate by 1.75% through 2024 and 2025. From late February to early April, oil prices rose more than 76%, pushing the Fed back into a more cautious stance. The Middle East conflict is now the binding constraint on Fed policy. Oil prices set the tone; the Fed follows.

Why it matters

The Fed's ability to cut rates in 2026 is hostage to oil. Any relief to inflation depends on Middle East developments, not monetary policy action. The Fed is in a holding pattern, not steering.

🇺🇸 USA
The idea: Rule of law versus executive discretion in trade policy

US courts keep striking down Trump tariffs. The administration is shifting tactics, not policy.

On May 7, 2026, the Court of International Trade ruled that the Section 122 tariffs are invalid. This is the second major court setback in as many months. On February 20, 2026, the Supreme Court ruled that the President cannot use IEEPA to impose tariffs. But the Trump administration is undeterred. As a result, the administration has moved to reconstruct the tariff wall through an expanded use of Sections 301 and 232. While existing Section 232 tariffs remain in place, it has launched two major Section 301 investigations aimed at maintaining what officials describe as "virtually unchanged tariff revenue" in 2026: A Section 301 investigation into structural manufacturing overcapacity covering sixteen countries and more than 75 percent of US imports · A Section 301 investigation into forced-labor enforcement practices across sixty economies, covering nearly all US imports. The legal battles continue, but the policy intent is fixed: maintain tariff walls through whatever authority survives judicial review.

Why it matters

US tariff policy is in legal limbo, but the economic impact will persist. Companies face constant uncertainty about which tariffs will survive the next court decision. The administration's tactical shift to Section 301 investigations suggests tariffs will remain at elevated levels throughout 2026, creating ongoing friction in supply chains.

Plain-English glossary

Selic
Brazil's overnight interbank lending rate, set by the Copom committee; Brazil's equivalent of the US Fed Funds rate.
IPCA
Brazil's broad national consumer price index, the inflation measure the Central Bank uses as its nominal anchor.
Copom
Brazil's Monetary Policy Committee, part of the Central Bank, which meets roughly every six weeks to set the Selic rate.
Real interest rate
The policy rate minus expected inflation; it measures the true purchasing power cost or return of borrowing or saving.
EUV (extreme ultraviolet lithography)
Advanced chip manufacturing technology used to etch circuits at cutting-edge nodes; restricted from export to China under US controls.
DUV (deep ultraviolet lithography)
Older semiconductor manufacturing technology, less advanced than EUV but sufficient for mature-node production; available to China for some applications.
